# The Puddle That Stopped an Army: v1 (Storm Oil form)

**Form.** Same as Myeongnyang v3: an anonymous "he", an invented and exaggerated plot, and a true "You see"
mechanism. No real person is named. The only real-world anchor is the "You see" line (the Dutch, 1672, a French
invasion), kept apart from the invented farm, general and cavalry.
- **True and stated** (queue section 3, facts 1-3):
  - in 1672 Holland flooded its own land against the French invasion, and the flood stopped the French short of
    Holland (fact 1: TWG 2023, Britannica);
  - the water was kept about knee-deep on purpose (about 40 cm; sources give 30-60 cm, so the narration says only
    "about knee-deep" and states no centimetre figure): too deep to wade through with horses and cannon, too shallow for boats (fact 2:
    Erfgoedhuis Zuid-Holland, TWG 2023);
  - the ditches between the fields could no longer be seen (fact 2: Amsterdam's resolution of 23 June 1672, "one
    cannot tell the ditches between the fields");
  - farmers fought the flooding and pumped their land dry with windmills (fact 3), used here as the neighbour's
    in-story act (not stated as fact on air).
- **Invented:** the farmer, his knee gauge, the neighbour and the cabbages, the laughing general, the vanishing
  horses and the hat, the stuck boats, the general carrying his boat home. The frost and thaw (fact 4) are left for
  a sequel, so no contested date is used.
- **Numbers:** 1672 is the only stated number (the judge also counts "one hat": 2 tokens).

**Title (T6).** "The Puddle That Stopped an Army": a concrete object (the general's own jeer), the mechanism (depth
and hidden ditches) hidden. Alternative: "The Farmer Who Flooded His Own Fields".

**Hook (Rick shape):** "He [flooded his own farm], with [an invading army] so close [he could hear their horses
snorting]."
- ASR-safe: no homophone pairs (no "dike/dyke", "strait/straight", "horse/hoarse" risk in context); the body says
  "floodgate" and never "dike".
- **Frame 0:** him close, cranking a wooden floodgate open, water gushing onto his green fields.
- **At 2-4 s:** the army on the road behind him: banners, cavalry, the horses' breath steaming.

**Running gag:** his knee as the depth gauge (trousers rolled to the knee, "Not yet", "That'll do", the knee check
and nod), paid off by the general going home "soaked to the knee".

**Mocker proved wrong:** the neighbour ("watering the cabbages", then pumps it out with a windmill and quietly stops
pumping) and the general ("defending their country with puddles"), who ends carrying his own boat.

**Stress marks.** Writer-marked for `melody: rules`: the gauge (KNEE, every time), the joke words (CABBAGES,
PUDDLES, HAT), the strange object (WINDMILL, BOAT), the mechanism's key words (SHALLOW, DITCH), VANISHES
and SOAKED; the hook's act verb (FLOODED) comes from the rules. Vetoed: speech and routine verbs (says, laughs,
sends, checks), particles (back, out, then), "enemy's" so the jeer lands on CABBAGES, and "water" (it is in
almost every line, so stressing it would flatten the contrast).

## Narration

```narration
[sfx: creak] He flooded his own farm, with an invading army so close he could hear their horses snorting.

[sfx: knock] His neighbour drops his pitchfork. "The enemy's coming, and you're watering the cabbages?"

[sfx: splash] He just props the floodgate open, rolls his trousers to the knee, and wades in.

[sfx: knock] "Not yet," he says, as the water creeps up his shin.

[sfx: crowd] By morning, the whole village is furious, and his neighbour is pumping it back out with a windmill.

[sfx: tick] Then the water touches his knee, and he slams the floodgate shut. "That'll do."

[sfx: drums] Soon the army reaches the flood, and their general laughs so hard he nearly falls off his horse.

[sfx: knock] "They're defending their country with puddles!"

[sfx: whoosh_long] The general sends his cavalry galloping across the shiny water.

[sfx: splash] Seconds later, the first horse vanishes, rider and all, leaving one hat floating. Then another.

[sfx: knock] The general sends boats to fish them out, and the boats scrape to a stop in the mud.

[sfx: tick] Back at the gate, the farmer checks the water against his knee, and his neighbour quietly stops pumping.

[sfx: whoosh] You see, the Dutch really did this in 1672 to stop a French invasion, keeping the water about knee-deep on purpose.

That's too deep to march horses and cannon through, but too shallow to float a boat.

And under it, every ditch between the fields disappears, so any step could be a plunge.

[sfx: impact] By sunset, the general who laughed at puddles is wading home, soaked to the knee, carrying his own boat.
```
