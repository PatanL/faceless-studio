### Pacing and device notes for the PLAYBOOK
- **~251 wpm for 10 minutes** with short lines (median ~7 words). The long form does not slow down; it adds more turns, not longer lines.
- **Accuse and counter-accuse:** two suspects each accuse the other with a mirrored reaction line ("my heart …, what do you mean?"). Cheap to animate (two private conversations) and doubles the mystery.
- **Competing talismans:** each suspect gives the hero an object. The viewer's question "which one is real?" carries the second half with no new characters.
- **Rules from an outside helper** make exposition feel like advice. The same helper is the hidden villain, so his rules can mislead (the mark rule frames the innocent).
- **The knowledge slip** is the cleanest fair-play twist in the corpus: one sentence, checkable by the viewer, and the hero explains it right after.
- **Nested dreams with a clock check:** a fixed time read on the phone after each waking. The number repeats, so the repetition is the scare. It fills 20% of the runtime with three set pieces and no new plot.
- **Red herrings with numbers** (the scar from "five days ago" matching the start of the sounds) work because the coincidence is concrete. They are never explained, and the viewers don't seem to mind.
- Plot hole worth noting: the pendant seems to protect him in the dreams (5:41) even though the brooch is the "real protection". The story never explains it, and it didn't hurt the views.

## 3. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Percentages are of runtime. Targets: works as a 3–4 min single episode (compress the dream block to one loop) or as 8–10 min long form. Keep a re-hook at most every ~10 s (median 9 s), and swing the suspicion between suspects every 30–40 s.

1. **Hook (0–2%, ≤7 s, ~30–35 words).** An exact hour, an unpleasant sound or sign at a boundary of the hero's room, which disappears whenever the hero checks, and a count of how many nights it has happened.
2. **Outside helper and rules (2–8%).** The hero asks for help somewhere public (a forum, a call-in, a stranger). The helper names the creature, inflates the stakes to world scale, and gives a deadline in days, a plant about how it attacks (an odd phrase that will become a whole act), and a "it hides when watched" rule. The hero voices disbelief once.
3. **Test 1 fails (8–12%).** A hidden recording device in a specific prop. The hero is put to sleep unnaturally, the footage shows nothing, the hero accuses the helper, and the helper gives an unfalsifiable excuse.
4. **Turn inward (12–16%).** The helper adds that the creature disguises itself as someone close. Back-fill the setting: a temporary home, a recent death, only two other people. Name suspect 1 with a rhetorical question and one recalled detail; suspect 2 arrives that day, out of character.
5. **Accusation 1 (16–22%).** Suspect 1 takes the hero aside and accuses suspect 2 with a folklore "proof" that is never checked. A third adult (with a unit number or address) confirms it and gives the hero protective object A.
6. **Test 2 promised (22–26%).** The helper describes a binary physical test with a color result. The hero objects; the helper says do it during sleep. The hero wonders if the real relative is already gone.
7. **Accusation 2, mirrored (26–31%).** Suspect 2 walks in at that moment and accuses suspect 1 with a habit-break clue (this is the real clue), plus a warning dream. Same reaction phrasing as beat 5. Object B is handed over. The hero says he trusts no one.
8. **Test 2 blocked (31–36%).** A slow sneak to suspect 1's bedside; the eyes open at the last instant; a stammered excuse; suspect 2 is awake too. The test is never done.
9. **Dream 1 (36–43%).** The recording device again, instant sleep, a false awakening with lost time, a partial glimpse of the creature from a doorway, a witness who sees nothing, a whisper from above that uses a plural, the full image overhead, then waking.
10. **Evidence (43–49%).** The footage now shows the creature. The helper asks for the hero's location (a sinister note, left alone) and gives a new rule: a mark that survives the disguise. The hero finds the mark on the footage.
11. **False match (49–54%).** The mark appears on suspect 2. His innocent explanation includes a number that matches the start of the haunting. Suspect 2 pleads to leave and reports seeing suspect 1 doing the creature's signature act.
12. **True clue (54–58%).** Suspect 1 has no mark, but repeats the habit break from beat 7. An emotional backstory makes the break impossible for the real person.
13. **Nested-dream trap (58–78%).** Forced sleep. Object A fights back and is damaged. The hero checks a fixed time on waking; it contradicts itself; the creature appears at the hook's boundary. Two then three layers deep. Reality tests (small pain, then big pain) fail. The worst image (suspect 2 doing something grotesque) is a dream lie. The hero tries to reach the third adult and is stopped at the door. On the last waking, both objects are damaged.
14. **The slip (78–83%).** The helper messages at the low point: destroy object B, keep object A, and names who gave object A, which the hero never told him. A beat of "something felt wrong", the realization, and a secret swap.
15. **Climax (83–89%).** Back in the dream, the creature gloats and attacks. Object B blasts it. The creature's own protest confirms it expected the swap. The hero explains the slip aloud, then names the real culprit: the first accuser.
16. **Reality and kill (89–96%).** The hero wakes and says this time it is real. The culprit is shown fully transformed and already restrained by suspect 2, who reveals a protective secret identity that explains his odd arrival. The hero delivers the kill with object B. A time jump to ash.
17. **Coda (96–100%).** "It's not over"; the third adult is exposed and dealt with in one line (paying off the plural whisper). A one-sentence new status quo, then an explicit end.

**Rules for reuse:**
- Give each suspect one "proof" against the other and one object. The real clue is the emotional one, not the physical one.
- The villain's tell must be a single fact the viewer heard the hero *not* say. Then have the hero explain it in one line.
- Swing suspicion at least 8 times across the runtime; never let the viewer settle for more than ~40 s.
- Make the dream block repeat a fixed number (a time, a count) so the repetition itself becomes the scare.
- Close the big loops for a complete episode; leave only one series hook in the coda.
- Use your own creature, objects, tests and wording. Borrow only the structure and devices.
