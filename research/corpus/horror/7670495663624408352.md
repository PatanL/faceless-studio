**Key mechanic, two rival authorities:** an authority figure (the dorm supervisor) explains away the hook's oddity, labels the roommate a monster and hands the narrator protective rules. Each rule backfires. Then the "monster" roommate turns out to be the real expert. She flips the supervisor's rules into their exact opposites ("keep your eyes closed" becomes "ghosts fear open eyes"). She proves it with a physical clue the narrator can touch: bone in the "blessed" ash. Then she shows outside evidence on a phone: a news story about a fire in **their** room number. The motive is a grieving mother who wants a replacement for her dead daughter. In the last 15 s the new ally takes the supervisor's place in the structure. She declares it "too late", issues a new rule of her own and ends on "it'll choose someone else". The viewer is left with one question: is Lily the next fake authority? The picture backs this up, because Lily's eyes are never shown.

There are two ladders. The sensory ladder (sound, approach, voice, touch) peaks at the midpoint. The information ladder (lie, remains, motive, our room) then takes over, so the second half is driven by reveals instead of scares.

### How it ends

- It ends mid-scene on a held static shot of Lily, with the final caption about the ghost choosing someone else. There is no CTA, no "part 2" card and no music sting in the frames.
- The emotional state at the cut is relief turning into renewed dread:
  - the villain is known and the scare has been survived,
  - but half her life force is gone, a quest is pending, and the cost will be passed to someone else.
- The structural echo (plea, then "listen carefully", then ritual, then "ghosts fear it") quietly casts the new ally in the old fake authority's role. That ambiguity, plus Lily's permanently hidden eyes, is what drives the comments and the series pull.

## 3. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Use this for a 2:45–3:00 first-person "two rival authorities" rules horror, at about 210 wpm, about 600 words. It works as a standalone with a Part 2 hook.

1. **Hook, 0–5% (about 28 words, 8 s):**
   - One mundane object or behaviour belonging to a person close to the narrator, given a colour tag.
   - An authority figure reacts with fear.
   - The authority gives a death-linked origin for the object.
   - The authority accuses the close person of being non-human, in "you" form.
   - The narrator asks a short plea question.
   - Visual: the object full-frame with a glitch, then the authority pointing into the lens.
2. **Rule 1, 5–8%:**
   - A 2-word "memorise this" cue.
   - One odd, visual ritual using everyday objects.
   - A stated protective effect.
3. **Backfire, 8–10%:**
   - The narrator obeys exactly.
   - Something impossible and personal goes wrong (a possession or a space disappears).
4. **Return to the authority, 10–27%:**
   - The narrator accuses the authority and briefly doubts her own humanity.
   - The authority's face darkens, and she gives a convenient explanation that blames the suspect.
   - She pre-empts disbelief and hands over a "sacred" protective object.
   - Rule 2: do not look or do not act, "no matter what happens".
   - The narrator asks what will happen; the authority refuses to say and gives a deadline of tonight.
   - The object seems to work (false relief).
5. **Night wait, 27–37%:**
   - Restate the open question twice.
   - An exact clock time and sleeplessness.
   - The suspect moves: a sound cue first (the colour-tagged object), then footsteps, then a counted ritual sound.
6. **Ordeal, 37–50%:**
   - The suspect calls the narrator by name, gets anxious and gives an order that contradicts rule 2.
   - The narrator dismisses it with a small piece of everyday logic.
   - That logic is instantly disproved by a disgusting physical sensation she cannot look at (the midpoint peak).
   - A one-line body reaction.
7. **Counter-reveal, 50–63%:**
   - The suspect explains why rule 2 exists, and it is the exact inverse of what was claimed.
   - The narrator gets a flash of suspicion ("how does she know?"), answered immediately: the same trick was played on her.
   - The protective object is really something grotesque tied to the authority's own loss.
   - The authority's goal is to replace that loss with the narrator.
   - The suspect predicts a physical detail the narrator can check.
8. **Proof, 63–69%:**
   - The narrator breaks rule 2 on purpose.
   - She confirms the grotesque detail by touch, and the sensation stops: the inverted rule works.
9. **Outside evidence, 69–84%:**
   - A phone shows a news item: a past death at a specific building and room number.
   - A photo of the grieving authority.
   - A short reaction line.
   - "That's our room."
   - A 3-step motive chain (loss, then a position of trust, then a plan).
10. **Hook object explained, 84–90%:**
    - The colour-tagged object was protection all along, with a credential (a relative with expertise) and one gross folk-ritual detail.
    - The villain is one step ahead.
11. **Mirror ending, 90–100%:**
    - The narrator repeats the hook's plea.
    - "Too late", with a quantified loss (a fraction of something vital).
    - The new ally repeats the rule-intro cue and gives a new ritual quest for tomorrow, using the same "they fear X" formula the fake authority used.
    - The last line implies survival means the threat moves to someone else.
    - Hold a static shot of the ally with her eyes hidden, then hard cut. No CTA.

**Devices to reuse:**
- an ordinary object as the hook, with a colour tag
- authority fear as proof
- rules that backfire when obeyed
- a "no matter what you hear" loop with a deadline
- the narrator voicing the viewer's question 2–3 times
- a contradictory order from the suspect, so the viewer must pick a side
- everyday logic disproved by sensation
- rule inversion
- a sacred object that is really remains
- a check-it-yourself prediction
- a phone news screen with a room-number callback
- the hook object explained last
- a mirror ending that casts the ally in the villain's role
- the ally's eyes never shown

**Pacing targets:**
- A spike every 4–5 s; never let a gap run past 11 s.
- 3–5 word punch lines at reversals.
- Put the sensory peak at about 45–50%, the biggest twist at about 60% and the evidence reveal at about 70–75%.
- Close most loops inside the video, but leave the survival and trust loop open.
- Cut the picture every 3–5 s. If the final dialogue uses one long static shot, keep it under 20 s or add slow push-ins and prop inserts.
