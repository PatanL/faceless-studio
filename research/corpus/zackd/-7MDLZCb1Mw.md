Fact check (redo it for any script of ours that borrows a claim). Three-toed sloths (*Bradypus*) have cracked, grooved outer hairs that soak up water and grow green algae (mainly *Trichophilus*). Sloth moths (*Cryptoses*) live in the fur as adults. About once a week the sloth climbs to the ground to defecate, and female moths lay eggs in the fresh dung. The larvae are coprophagous; when the adults emerge they fly up to find a sloth. Pauli et al. (2014) proposed that moths carry nutrients (nitrogen from their bodies and waste, broken down in the fur) that feed the algae. They also proposed that sloths may **eat** the algae as a supplement. The video uses camouflage as the benefit, which is the older and less tested hypothesis, and it leaves out the eating hypothesis. "May even" is a fair hedge. The video also leaves out the irony that the trip to the ground is the most dangerous thing a sloth does, since a large share of predation happens there.

## 2. Escalation ladder

This is a **cycle** ladder, not a single-mechanism ladder. Each rung adds an actor or a step, and the last step joins back to the first.

1. **Problem** (0.0 s): the fur is infested. Mild disgust.
2. **Reassurance** (4.3 s): it may not be bad. Curiosity: how could it be good?
3. **First benefit** (7.3 s): colour matches the trees, so the sloth is camouflaged. The stakes appear (predators).
4. **The cost** (14.4 s): the sloth has to go down to the ground to poop. Comic gross-out, and the scene moves from canopy to forest floor.
5. **Reproduction** (16.5 s): eggs go into the dung. The second actor gets a job.
6. **Gross peak** (19.0 s): larvae eat the dung. This is the money beat at about 65% of runtime, though it is comedic rather than visceral.
7. **Return** (21.1 s): adults fly back up into the fur. The direction reverses: down, then up.
8. **Circle closes** (24.4-27.5 s): dead moths feed the algae. The two actors from the hook turn out to depend on each other. This is the conceptual peak.
9. **Thesis** (27.8-30.5 s): the whole loop protects the sloth. The ladder resolves into one sentence.

Shape: act 1 (problem, turn and benefit, 0-13.9 s, 46%) is slow and spacious. Act 2 (the four-step cycle, 14.4-24.0 s, 31%) is rushed, with 33 words in 9.6 s. Act 3 (close and thesis, 24.4-30.5 s, 20%) slows a little. Breaths of 0.3-0.5 s fall at every sentence end. There is no explicit re-hook line. ## 3. Punchline and twist mechanics

- **Reframe given away early.** The flip ("not a bad thing") comes at 4.3 s (14%), not at about two-thirds through as in the cookiecutter outlier. After that the viewer is not waiting to find out *what* is true, only *how*. This keeps people watching but lowers the payoff, because there is no late surprise that changes what was already seen.
- **Closed-loop reveal as the twist.** The real twist is structural: the last cycle step (dead moths feed algae) links the two nouns from the hook. The "may even" intensifier marks it. The pleasure is "everything connects", not "I was wrong".
- **Recurring gross word as comedy.** "Poop" is said twice in about 5 s, with "pile" in between. Disgust is played for a laugh with a cute animal, which keeps it safe for all ages. The dung is also where the channel hides its logo (section 6), a wink that rewards the gross beat.
- **Two hedges set the tone.** "Not necessarily" and "may even" make the narrator sound careful and accurate. They also take stakes away; neither hedge appears in the cookiecutter outlier.
- **Explicit thesis button.** The ending explains ("meaning the whole system...") rather than leaving an understated, ominous line. It feels finished, which may lower replays. The **visual** loop is strong, though: the first and last frames are both a wide, low-angle shot of the sloth hanging from a diagonal branch against the sky.
- **Callback by echoing nouns.** "Sloth's fur" (opening, 21 s), "algae" (opening, 7 s, 27 s) and predators/protect (13 s, 29 s) each come back. The script is built from a small set of nouns, which makes a dense cycle easy to follow at 210+ wpm.

## 6. Visual grammar notes

- **Character design.** The look is photoreal 3D (groomed hair simulation) with one bit of character acting: the sloth's natural face markings read as a smile, and in the reassurance shot it tilts its head and closes its eyes like a contented pet. The moths are anatomically plausible, with large striped, translucent wings. The larva is cute rather than revolting: plump, translucent, peach-coloured, a little like a shrimp. The predators are almost symbols. The first is an unnaturally red raptor that reads as a threat instantly against the cyan sky. The second is grey and harpy-like. Neither ever touches the sloth.
- **Palette.** Three worlds, each with its own palette. (1) Canopy: a saturated cyan sky gradient, almost a studio backdrop, against leaf green and the sloth's beige-grey. (2) Microscopic fur: warm khaki tubes and glossy green algae, with one cool blue "science" shot at 2.0 s and an acid-green glow at 26.3 s. (3) Forest floor and dung: near-black brown under a warm key light, with pearly white eggs and peach larvae for contrast. Green is the thread that runs through all three, and it carries the "camouflage" idea.
- **Lighting.** Canopy shots are high-key daylight with a bright sky "halo" behind the subject (the sky is brightest right behind the sloth), so silhouettes read at a glance. Macro shots use a shallow depth of field, rim light on the hair ridges and moth wings, and soft bloom. The dung shots use a single warm key on a dark surround, like a product shot.
- **Materials.** Hair at macro scale is drawn like bark or bamboo, which quietly rhymes with "same color as the trees". Algae are glossy subsurface clumps, like moss or broccoli florets. The eggs are pearly and specular. The dung is a matte, rock-like boulder. Moth death is a particle dissolve. The materials are clean and tactile throughout, and nothing is wet or bloody.
- **Camera language.** There are four registers: (1) low angle looking up at the sloth against the sky, the default; (2) a slow lateral track across microscopic terrain; (3) a worm's-eye macro on the small actors (moth, eggs, larva); (4) a big vertical move for the plot (crane down for "climbs down", tilt up for "fly back"). Scale changes always go through a motion-blur crash-zoom or whip (0.45, 4.25, 24.05, 26.86 s), so the video "dives" four times. A passing wing works as a wipe at 13.3 s.
- **Cut rhythm.** Act 1 holds shots for 2-3 s (face 2.2 s, arm and bark 3.3 s, predator flyby with crane 3.0 s). Act 2 speeds up to 0.3-1.4 s shots during the fastest speech (5 shots in 18.25-22.8 s). The last wide shot is held for 2.8 s. Cuts land on phrase boundaries. Apart from the four scale whips and the wing wipe, all cuts are hard cuts.
- **Caption style.** White, heavy, rounded sans-serif with a soft drop shadow and no box. Centred at about 78% of frame height. Cards hold 1-4 words, split on phrase boundaries, in mostly lower case; sentence-initial words keep their capital ("And when the", "When they die"), and apostrophes are kept ("sloth's"). There is no word highlight and no colour emphasis. One card does not match the voice: "Now as they mature" adds a "Now" that is not spoken, so the captions are typed separately from the voice-over rather than auto-generated from it.
- **Branding as a gag.** The channel's only logo is modelled into the dung pellet as embossed text (16.0-18.3 s), right where the gross beat lands. It is part of the scene, so it also makes cropped re-uploads harder. There is no corner watermark, intro or outro.

## 7. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Format: the "gross but useful" symbiosis cycle. One loved animal, a disgusting fact about it, an early promise that the fact is good for it, then a closed life cycle of 4-5 steps whose last step feeds the first actor. Target 80-90 words, 31 s, about 165 wpm, 22-26 shots. To beat this control, keep the cycle but take the fixes in section 8.

Checks before using this skeleton:
- Name both small actors in the hook so the closing link reads as a callback.
- Use at most 5-6 nouns in the whole script and reuse them; a dense cycle at 200+ wpm only works with recurring words.
- Keep the speaking rate within 155-180 wpm in every sentence. Do not rush the cycle (this control ran at 216).
- Each cycle step gets exactly one shot with one move. Location changes get a vertical move, and scale changes get a crash-zoom.
- Make sure the claimed benefit is the best-supported one, and mention the competing hypothesis in our fact notes.

## 8. Why this is a control and not an outlier (compared with `CRGWDiIgMZ4`, same cluster)

These are hypotheses from two videos, n=2. Test them against the other controls before treating them as rules.

| Lever | Cookiecutter (4.07x, 58M) | Sloth (1.00x, 14.6M) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Action already happening, with a pronoun ("as it...") | A declarative fact with the subject named |
| When the reframe lands | 66% of runtime: a late twist reinterprets what was shown | 14%: the flip is announced up front, then explained |
| Money shot | One satisfying-geometry image held for about 5 s | Spread over 5 cycle steps of equal weight; no single image to remember |
| Stakes | Predator and prey, a wound | Mild: camouflage, poop comedy, hedged twice |
| Ending | Understated threat that grammatically loops into line 1 | An explicit "meaning..." thesis; the loop is visual only |
| Pace | Even, 164-177 wpm | Uneven, 137-216 wpm |
| Engagement | 1.56% likes: driven by replays | 3.0% likes: liked but not replayed as much |

The likely lesson for our nature episodes: keep the reveal until late, build to one striking image, and make the last line run into the first. A cute animal and a cycle structure produce likes, but they do not by themselves produce replays.
