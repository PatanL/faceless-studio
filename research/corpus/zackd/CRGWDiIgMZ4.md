Fact check (redo it for any script of ours that borrows a claim): *Isistius brasiliensis* is about 40-55 cm long. Its upper teeth are small and narrow; its lower teeth are large triangular blades that interlock into one saw-edged band. It latches on with suctorial lips, then spins its body to cut out a round plug about 5 cm wide. Crater scars from these bites are found on whales, dolphins, tuna, large squid and even submarine sonar domes. The video **leaves out the suction-lip step** and shows the bite as teeth only. "Evolved to take just enough" is fair shorthand for a facultative ectoparasite, but the framing as restraint is anthropomorphic.

## 2. Escalation ladder

Each rung raises either the scale of what moves or the stakes of what it means. Nothing repeats.

1. **Contact** (0.0 s): the bite is already happening; the stakes are implied.
2. **Small part** (1.7 s): tiny upper teeth anchor the bite. Low intensity, so there is headroom.
3. **Big part** (4.3 s): huge lower teeth, compared to a blade. The weapon is revealed.
4. **Whole body** (7.9 s): the action grows from one part to the entire animal rotating. This is the peak of the motion.
5. **Removal** (10.9 s): a perfect round plug comes out. This is the peak of the gross-out (the money shot).
6. **Aftermath** (14.2 s): the victim swims off with a neat hole. A small surprise: it survives.
7. **Reason** (18.2 s): small cannot kill big. The intellectual turn.
8. **Strategy and recurrence** (21.7-27.0 s): it takes a little and comes back. The meaning flips, and the line hands off to the loop.

Shape: rungs 1-5 escalate physically (act 1 mechanism, 0-7.5 s; act 2 result, 7.9-17.8 s). Rungs 6-8 escalate conceptually (act 3 reason, 18.2-27.0 s). The split is roughly 27% / 36% / 32%, with breaths as the only act breaks. There is no mid-video re-hook line; the cut to a new, more extreme image every 1-3 s does that job.

## 3. Punchline and twist mechanics

- **Answer-first title contract.** The title asks "How...?" and the video answers from frame 0 with no preamble, no "did you know" and no channel intro. The viewer is paid off within 2 s and keeps watching to see the next step.
- **Pronoun delay.** "It" is used for 8 s before "the shark" is named. This works only because the title and thumbnail supply the noun, and it saves the hook 2-3 words.
- **Twist = reframe, not new fact.** The twist adds nothing new to the picture. It reinterprets what was already shown: an apparent attack is a deliberate partial meal. The hinge is the conversational "You see," at 66% of the runtime.
- **Double size contrast.** Small/huge (teeth, act 1) sets up small/big (animals, act 3). The same contrast first describes how the bite works and then explains why it happens, so the ending feels earned.
- **Button doubles as the loop seam.** The final clause is an understated threat about recurrence. The first clause is a dangling "As it..." that grammatically continues it. The final shot shows the shark cruising toward a fresh target, which matches the opening bite, so the restart feels seamless. This is probably why replays are high while likes and comments are low.
- **Satisfying geometry.** "Perfectly round" and "clean" turn violence into a satisfying-shape payoff, which keeps it within the audience's tolerance. The camera lingers longest on the plug and the hole (about 5 s in total).

## 6. Visual grammar notes

- **Character design.** The look is semi-real 3D: anatomically believable sharks, whales and squid, with one cute exaggeration, a large glossy jewel-like eye on the shark that reads as alive and a bit sly. There are no faces, expressions or text bubbles. The victim (the whale) is almost never shown whole in close shots; it works as a wall or terrain, which sells its scale.
- **Palette.** Two colours dominate: a near-monochrome cobalt/teal ocean (background, about 70% of pixels) against a warm salmon/peach animal and saturated red flesh. This is the orange/blue complementary split, so the subject always pops. The blood is deep crimson and kept to stylized ink clouds.
- **Lighting.** Sunlight comes from the surface, with caustic ripples on the whale's skin and on the hole. Warm golden rim light picks out tooth edges and the plug's rim. Volumetric haze makes the wide shots fade to dark blue at the bottom. Close-ups have a shallow depth of field.
- **Materials.** Wet, subsurface-scattered skin; glassy specular teeth; the flesh plug is marbled like raw salmon steak with a fat rim. It looks like food, which keeps the gross-out "satisfying". Small floating particles and bubbles in every shot sell the water.
- **Camera language.** Everything is slow and smooth CG, with no shake. There are four registers: (1) macro on the mechanism part, (2) low angle looking up at the subject, (3) top-down for the action diagram, (4) extreme wide for the scale payoff. Shots move from macro to wide as the story moves from mechanism to consequence. Each shot has one slow move: a push-in on reveals, a follow on travel.
- **Cut rhythm.** Mechanism shots are held long (2-4 s) so the viewer can read the anatomy. After the payoff the cuts speed up to about 0.5-1.5 s on travel and aftermath shots. Cuts land on phrase boundaries, never mid-word. There are no transitions other than hard cuts (soft scene scores of 0.03-0.11, because every shot shares the blue). The one motion-graphic element is the chevron rotation ring, a single diagram overlay in a light tint that matches the palette.
- **Caption style.** White, heavy rounded sans-serif in sentence case, with a soft drop shadow and no box or stroke. Centered horizontally at about 78% of frame height (lower third, clear of the subject). Cards hold 2-4 words, split on natural phrase boundaries. There is no word-by-word highlight, no colour emphasis and no emoji. Punctuation is dropped except apostrophes. Card timing follows the voice exactly.
- **No branding.** There is no watermark, intro, outro card or CTA. The emoji is in the title only.

## 7. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Format: one creature's single strange mechanism, told answer-first in under 30 s as three acts with one reframe, closed by a loop. Target 70-80 words, about 165-175 wpm, 15-18 shots.

| % of runtime | Beat | What to write (slots, not words) | What to show |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-6% | COLD | A dangling subordinate clause ("While / As [pronoun] [verb]s the [victim]..."). Leave the subject to the title; start mid-action | Extreme close-up of the subject at the moment of contact; the victim as a wall filling half the frame |
| 6-15% | MECH 1 | Smallest part of the mechanism + a body verb | Macro of that part acting |
| 15-27% | MECH 2 | Biggest part + a contrast adjective + a one-word everyday-object simile; end the sentence and breathe | Macro with rim light, framed so it looks like the object in the simile |
| 29-40% | MECH 3 | "Next/Then" + the whole subject does one big motion | Top-down; one simple overlay diagram (arrows, ring) for the motion |
| 40-52% | PAYOFF | The result as a precise shape or number (perfect circle, exact count) + a kitchen or workshop verb | Hold and push in on the result shown face-on; the longest hold of the video |
| 52-64% | AFTER | The victim's unexpected fate (survives, does not notice, is unharmed) + a neatness word; breathe | Extreme wide for scale, then a centered close-up of the mark left behind |
| 66-80% | REFRAME | A conversational turn marker + the size or power contrast that explains why ("one this [small] can't [verb] something that [big]") | The subject alone in empty space, then next to a second, different giant (silently showing the habit is general) |
| 80-90% | TWIST | Strategy in 6-8 words: what it does instead; then a 2-word punch | The mechanism repeats on the second victim, in brief |
| 90-100% | BUTTON / LOOP | An understated, mildly ominous recurrence clause whose last words lead grammatically into the opening clause | The subject approaches a fresh target with the same framing and motion as frame 0 |

Checks before using this skeleton:
- Name the subject in the title, not the first line. The first spoken words must be action.
- Use the same contrast pair twice: once for how it works, once for why.
- One overlay graphic at most, used only for a motion that is hard to read.
- Read the last line straight into the first line out loud; it must sound like one sentence.
- The visual must not just illustrate. At least once it should add something the voice does not say (here, the second victim).
