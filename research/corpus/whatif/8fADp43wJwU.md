Known caption quirks: "Svartsengi Island geothermal plant" is the Svartsengi plant in Iceland (the stray "Island" is probably a caption slip); the 0:41 and 4:12 sentences run over two caption lines (split at 0:51 and 4:23). The script appears to be adapted from the What If? essay on the same question ("Last Human Light"), so it is a written essay read aloud, which explains the long sentences and high wpm.

**Key mechanic:** an **elimination tournament with a lifespan on every candidate**. The title asks a single "when" question; the video answers it by lining up every kind of human light source and giving each one a death date and a cause of death. Candidates come in roughly rising order of lifespan (hours → days–months → years → decades → a century → centuries), so each new candidate is a re-hook ("does this one last longer?"). Most deaths share two named killers (grid failure, then corrosion / seized gearboxes), so later candidates die by callback. The apparent winner (remote solar lights) is crowned with a hedge ("if we follow a strict definition"), and then a "weird" wildcard from outside the obvious category (glowing glass blocks of nuclear waste) takes the title. The last line restates the question's answer as an ironic image: our most toxic waste is our last light.

## 3. Transferable skeleton

A template for a 4.5–5.5 min long-form "how long until the last X?" (or "what would be the last X to survive?") explainer answered by elimination. Structure only, no original wording.

1. **Question hook (0–3%).** Read a named viewer's question in full as sentence one, with a hand-wave for the cause of the premise so the video stays on the thing being timed. Keep it to one sentence, ~30 words, ≤ 10 s.
2. **Kill the obvious answer (3–13%).** "Start with the obvious": the biggest system fails first. Pre-empt the most common objection with a concession + "but" and a comic list item, name the human dependency in an understated line, then show the failure as a cascade with a time stamp.
3. **False hope, then crush it (13–23%).** Raise a contender that looks unbeatable, with first-hand expert testimony and one huge comparison ratio. Within ~15 s, turn it with "unfortunately" and a design rule, and have it die by a callback to step 2's failure.
4. **Roadmap pivot (23–25%).** One "however" line that reopens the question and promises a list of candidates, each with a death time. This tells the viewer the format and opens the per-candidate loop.
5. **Quick-fire ladder (25–56%).** 4–5 candidates, 10–30 s each, in roughly rising order of lifespan. Each rung: introduce → one piece of evidence (a document, a dated precedent, an operator quote, a spec number) → a lifespan with a range → a named cause of death. Early on, state a sorting rule that predicts the order (the less it needs people, the longer it lasts). Plant 2 shared killers (one mechanical, one chemical) and let later candidates die "of the same thing". Put one twist inside a rung (it still runs but does nothing useful). Add a bar-lowering gag that quietly redefines the category (even the tiniest indicator counts) to set up the ending.
6. **Front-runner and false ending (56–71%).** Name the "most promising candidate" with a hedge. Narrow from a list of examples to one concrete everyday object, list the conditions that could kill it, and give it a lifespan far beyond the rest. Let nature do the maintenance (wind, rain) as the logic for why it wins. Crown it provisionally with a definitional hedge that leaves the door open.
7. **Wildcard twist (71–95%).** "But there's another contender, and it's a weird one": bring back a category the viewer thought was eliminated, in an unexpected form. Raise the obvious objection yourself (a myth-bust), detour through a familiar near-answer and take it away for the same reason the other candidates died, then name the real phenomenon with an everyday simile, walk the process chain, and land the reveal as a short image at the end of a long sentence. Do the long-horizon maths (half-life or decay rate) that beats the front-runner, and add one honest nuance with an emotional edge.
8. **Answer button (95–97%).** Explicitly close the loop ("so here's the answer"), give the time scale, and end on an ironic image (the worst or least-loved thing we made is what outlasts us). Then end card, no CTA.

**Pacing targets:** a written-essay voice at ~200–225 wpm with ~20–25-word sentences and clause-final reveals (or ~150–170 wpm if the channel's TTS needs shorter lines). Hook ≤ 10 s / ~30 words. A new candidate or turn word every ~25 s (never more than ~45 s without one). A real, checkable piece of evidence every ~30–40 s in the ladder. Light deadpan jokes in the first half; wonder images in the second half. Front-runner named by ~55–60%, twist by ~70%, answer in the last 5%. Every lifespan gets a number or range, every death gets a named cause, and at least two causes are reused as callbacks.
