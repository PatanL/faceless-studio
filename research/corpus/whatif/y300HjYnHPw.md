**Key mechanic:** this is a "superpower stress-test" told as a story. One rule is changed (legs 10x) and one rule is explicitly kept ("brain and eyes stay normal"), then planted with "it matters later". The video spends 40% of its runtime enjoying and pricing the power, then pays off the planted rule as the real problem (you can't stop or turn). It solves that with a real-world analogue (the cheetah), stress-tests the solution in a life-or-death rescue, and then pivots into a recurring-universe recruitment cliffhanger ("Project IF"), so the episode ends on a series hook rather than a closed answer.

## 3. Transferable skeleton

Template for a long-form (8–11 min) second-person "What if you had [power × N]?" story-explainer, aimed at teens and gamers, at ~180 wpm. Structure only, no wording.

1. **Cold open, 0–2%.** Put "you" in a small, relatable deadline crisis. Use the power once to solve it instantly, in 2–3 short lines. A side character reacts with disbelief and gets a one-line punchline. Only then ask the title question. Keep it to about 40 words, 12 s.
2. **Rules card, about 2%.** State exactly one changed variable in concrete body terms. Explicitly list what stays normal (the hidden flaw). Tell the viewer to remember it because it matters later; this is the master open loop, closed at about 40% of runtime.
3. **Scale ladder, about 5%.** A personal baseline number → the multiplied number → 2–3 everyday comparisons, each bigger than the last → a famous real record, beaten by a wide margin. Show every number on screen.
4. **Demo scene, about 3%.** One short story scene where an authority figure tests you, with a two-stage gag (the reaction, then a topper line).
5. **"Why it's hard to believe" science, about 5%.** Real research framed as "scientists tested…", with a counter-intuitive one-line finding. State the human limit, then "yours doesn't". Add a sensory sound gag.
6. **Honeymoon montage, about 9%.** Open with an "at first" hedge. Give 4–5 perks of rising scale (personal → home → job → sport → city). Include one early crack inside the montage that foreshadows the real flaw, and call back the cold-open problem as solved.
7. **Numbered problem ladder, about 17%.** Open with "but then" and announce the first problem. Run three problems, ordered cosmetic → financial → biological. Each one gets a real formula or stat, a worked example with big numbers, a body-level consequence, a comic fix or family gag, and one pre-emptive myth-bust that answers the obvious comment-section question.
8. **Rug pull, about 12%.** Declare that none of that was the real problem. State the thesis in one antithesis line. Close the master loop by calling back the "stays normal" rule, then show the danger in numbers (reaction distance, stopping distance, turning radius) and dramatize it in two incidents: a lucky near-miss with an animal, then a harmless slapstick crash.
9. **Real-world analogue + training, about 8%.** Bring in an animal or machine that solves the problem in reality, flagged as "this part is real". Pull out the principle as a two-part aphorism. Show a short gear-and-training montage that ends with a stated "most important rule" (a warning plant), followed by a "for a while, it works" hedge.
10. **Climax test, about 13%.** A specific day and place, with a vulnerable third party in danger and a ticking threat whose easy out has been removed. Turn it into a numbers problem (distance, seconds). Spell out both failure modes, including one where the hero becomes the hazard. The hero applies the planted rule and the planted number, and the scene peaks on sensory detail. Release with a gentle payoff and a comic undercut, show the physical cost, and frame the event with an ironic name that states the moral.
11. **Universe twist, about 20%.** Same night, a mysterious stranger who already knows about the event, hinting at "all of you". Parade 4–6 characters from other episodes, about 6 s each, each with a visual tell and a gag; include one callback to a prop from this episode. Give the team a branded name tied to the channel, a recruitment line that redefines the hero's role, and a family-comedy beat for permission. Then an alarm: a known-powerful character is scared and a new antagonist cancels a known power, shown in a different visual register (CCTV).
12. **CTA, about 3%.** Leave the antagonist unresolved. Ask two comment questions: a personal "what would you do first" and a speculative "who is it". Ask for a subscribe with a series promise, and end on a fixed brand tagline. End card: the cliffhanger question over the full cast.

**Pacing targets:** ~180–190 wpm; 1–3 word punch line every 20–30 s; a verbal pivot or signpost every ~30–35 s; a comedy beat every 30–45 s; a switch between story world and blueprint explainer every 15–40 s; every statistic shown as a big on-screen number; at least 5 small plants that are called back later; master loop open for about 40% of runtime; the final loop left open into the CTA.
