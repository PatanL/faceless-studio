**Key mechanic:** a **counterfactual chain**. Take away one thing everyone treats as ordinary, then follow the consequences in order: biology (why we're naked) → prehistory (when clothes appeared) → migration (which continents stay empty) → body adaptations → housing → culture/identity → industry → computing → protective gear → space. Each step is pinned to one **real, checkable fact** (fur loss for sweat-running, the thermoneutral zone of ~27–32 °C, the clothing-louse split ~170 kyr ago, the ~50 kyr bone needle, out of Africa ~60–70 kyr, the Bering land bridge, British textile mills as the first factories, Jacquard's punched-card loom of 1804 → Babbage → punched-card data). The fiction is only the "so in this timeline…" step after each fact. The hook's three teases (no Europe/America, no phone, an invention you forgot was one) are each paid later in the order they were given. Facts check out at the level a viewer can check; "20° below freezing" for ice-age Europe and the "small fraction of 8 billion" estimate are loose.

## Transferable skeleton

A long-form (10–11 min, ~150 wpm, ~1,600–1,650 words) "what if [an everyday invention / trait] never existed?" alternate history. No wording from the source.

1. **0–7% Hook (≈45–50 s, ≈120 words).**
   - a. Read the title question aloud as line one.
   - b. Second-person immersion in the viewer's next morning, with a list of everyday objects that are missing, ending on the smallest one.
   - c. Two-step rule-out: it's not a theft or a disaster; it never existed in all of history.
   - d. Name the obvious joke angle in the viewer's voice, dismiss it, and promise bigger stakes.
   - e. **Three teases** of rising surprise: a big geographic or physical loss (with named places), a link to an object in the viewer's hand, and a thesis seed ("you forgot this was ever invented").
   - f. "Back to the beginning" signpost.
2. **7–22% Act 1, real history as mini-mysteries (2 chapters, ~45–55 s each).** Why we need the thing (a biology mechanism, a precise comfort or danger number with a unit conversion), then when it first appeared (one clever indirect proof: a "slightly gross" or detective-style method, plus one archaeological object). Each fact gets a tease → answer micro-loop of a few seconds. Give one tiny object outsized importance; it comes back in the last line.
3. **22–35% The fork (≈75 s).** "Now let's take it out of history." A real event (a migration or expansion) meets an obstacle. Use paired "in our timeline… / in this timeline…" lines, then a dark wordplay button. Pay hook tease 1 with a fast Q → one-word-answer list of places, the biggest saved for last with a map mechanism. End on a map picture and a paradox metaphor button.
4. **35–52% Physical world (2 chapters, ~60 s each).** Voice the viewer's "won't X just fix it?" objection and rebut it honestly. Then 2–3 grounded body or environment adaptations, with one "enemy you didn't see coming" re-hook. Then a shelter chapter opening with an "X isn't just a tool, it's life" line: what homes, villages and future cities look like, with an "in our world / in theirs" winter-type contrast and a one-word "Period." punch.
5. **52–63% Culture (≈70 s).** "It was never just about [function]; it was a language." Rapid anaphora list of role signals, a question ("how would you tell people apart?"), a paradox answer, a colour-code example, a modern-object simile, a "real peoples did this" reality check. Handle any sensitive angle abstractly, with a "not because… no, because…" construction and a "maybe it's…" triad.
6. **63–75% The two big surprises, back to back (~40 s + ~45 s).** "This is where it gets wild": a quiz question with two wrong guesses, then the surprising answer that links the invention to a historical revolution. Immediately "it gets stranger": a dated, named inventor, a mechanism explained plainly, an "in other words, it was a [modern word]" reframe, a 3-step chain to the present, and a family-metaphor payoff of hook tease 2. End with an honest hedge.
7. **75–77% Comic pattern interrupt (~10 s).** One everyday-life question, a quick funny answer list, then "that's the funny side; here's the serious side".
8. **77–85% Final climb (~45 s).** A rule-of-three list of professions that depend on the invention, a "who would even think of this?" question, then "the biggest one of all": reframe the highest achievement (space) as a form of the invention. Escalate the earlier trap callback from regional to planetary.
9. **85–93% Recap (~55 s, slower pace).** "Let's put it all together" + the title restated. Population, map, tech level, each a callback, plus one *new* surprise inside the recap. Turn to "but there's something beautiful too".
10. **93–98% Thesis (~30 s).** "We think of it as ordinary" + the viewer's everyday routine (bookend with the hook's morning) → "but the truth is…" reframe that pays the hook seed → anaphora montage calling back 4–5 chapters in order → a single human-scale sting combining the two thematic seeds.
11. **98–100% CTA (~15 s).** An on-theme action pointed at the viewer's own body or room right now, a short joke button, then subscribe + "the next one is even stranger".

**Pacing targets from this reference:** ~150 wpm, steady; hook ≤ 50 s with the premise in the first 3 s; the counterfactual itself starts by ~22% (real-science chapters first build trust); chapters 40–75 s with a one-line button each; a verbal re-hook or chapter question every ~30 s (never more than ~50 s); 3 hook teases paid in order of length (first by ~30%, the viewer's-object link by ~75%, the thesis seed by ~94%); the two biggest surprises stacked at 63–75% with escalating "wild → stranger" re-hooks; a 10 s comic interrupt before the last climb; recap + thesis + CTA ≈ 16% of runtime, the recap slowed to ~125 wpm.
