**Key mechanic:** the video is **inverted-perspective worldbuilding driven by dramatic irony**. "You" are a native of a floating undersea city, and the surface ("the ceiling") is the unknown. Ordinary things from our world are described by someone who has never seen them: diving seabirds are "fallers", rain is a word in a nursery rhyme that kids think is a fish, land is the blank white on a chart, a drowned forest is "old reef", a playground slide is a mysterious ramp that "only works if you can fall". The viewer decodes each clue before the hero does, so every reveal makes the viewer feel clever, and the decoding is the retention engine. Under it runs a simple quest (the hero's lifelong goal to find where the fallers go, set in the first 10 s) on a journey map (the grandfather's chart). Real ocean science (current layers, the nightly plankton migration, thermal power, blooms, fin whales) is folded into the story as everyday life, never as an aside to camera. The ending is a sensory first contact with our ordinary world (sky, birds, land, rain), closed with callbacks and a bookend vote.

## 3. Transferable skeleton

A template for an 8–11 min long-form second-person "what if humans lived in [alien setting]" worldbuilding story, where the alien world's natives meet something ordinary from ours. Structure only, no original wording.

1. **Riddle cold open (0–2%).** First sentence: an everyday thing from our world described by someone who has never understood it, with a precise frequency. Then "nobody knows [where/what]", then tie it to the hero's personal goal since childhood. Do not state the premise; let the viewer half-solve the riddle.
2. **Ordinary world through a humble job (2–12%).** Name the setting with a coined proper noun plus two round numbers (people, vessels/buildings). Give the hero a small, concrete job, and plant its key object with a precise count. Add 4–6 routine details that borrow modern-life words (commute, forecast, TV) and end each with a deadpan button. Plant one **fossil of our world** (a song, saying or word whose meaning is lost) with a wrong guess about it that can be reversed at the end.
3. **Mentor and the map (12–18%).** An elder keeps a record of the known world (map, logbook, catalogue) and promises it will pass to the hero. The record has one blank area the culture believes is empty. Show the fixed annual routine with a tactile image of repetition. Then the inciting incident: the resource moves toward the blank area.
4. **Departure with folded-in science (18–35%).** Explain how the world works with 2–3 real mechanisms, each as an analogy to a familiar thing, not a lecture. Plant a safety drill that will later be impossible. Teach the taboo about the unknown as a three-part childhood chant, plus the legend of the few who broke it, plus a narrator hedge that it is only true "around here". Add one night-wonder beat with a real phenomenon and a tender childhood memory (an object that comes back later). Close with a community ritual (a vote with a unique physical method) that will bookend the ending.
5. **Off the map (35–45%).** The routine breaks "for the first time anyone can remember". A ticking number on the status board, a real cost (power, warmth), then an abundance payoff with a real big-number animal or event. Re-raise the cold-open riddle: the mystery thing is now more frequent.
6. **The ground changes (45–57%).** The environment pushes the city toward the taboo. The hero investigates and brings back an artefact from our world, described without its name (the viewer decodes it). Give the elder a catch-all running gag, and one line of lore explaining why the past was forgotten (plausible, slightly unsettling). The hero volunteers for a dangerous scouting job.
7. **Drowned past (57–66%).** Midpoint reveal: geometry or design that proves people lived here. Use the hero's job expertise as the lens (the one object in it that differs from all the hero has known) and draw a short deduction from it. Add one physical-comedy artefact test that fails because of the new setting, ending in an aphorism that hints at the world beyond.
8. **Crisis (66–73%).** The planted safety drill can't work here. The city takes an unusual defensive action (one short line), the hero's own object breaks, and the hero repairs it in the dark because "it's my job". The elder watches through the glass. Resolve by morning.
9. **Threshold (73–80%).** The map runs out. State the taboo again, then the hero tests each part with evidence (a number on the board, a sensory observation). Put the two artefacts side by side; the elder's silence and the *absence* of his running gag is his change of heart. He hands over an object that sanctions the quest.
10. **Ascent and reveal (80–92%).** Repeat the cold-open event almost exactly, but now the hero follows. Slow down the pace and give the music room. Bust the three taboo items one by one, close the master riddle in two words, then a final sensory reveal of something ordinary to us, with a taste or touch. Bring back the fossil from step 2 and reverse its wrong guess.
11. **Homecoming (92–98%).** The elder repeats the hero's sensory action, hands over the record-keeping tool, and the hero fills the blank with a deep-time number ("first time in N years"). A wordless symbolic gesture from the elder ends the arc.
12. **Bookend button (last ~2%).** Repeat the community ritual from step 4 and tell its result only through sound or duration. No CTA, then the channel card.

**Pacing targets:** ~150–160 wpm (170+ through the first 70%, slowing to ~95–130 with music gaps for the ascent and ending). Riddle hook ≤ 12 s / ~30 words, world set up by ~30 s / ~90 words, premise never said aloud. A chapter turn ("Then…", day stamp, colour change) every 30–45 s. A dramatic-irony decode or a real-science fact every 20–30 s. A deadpan button every 30–45 s until the climax, then none. At least 5 long plants (> 4 min) closed in the last 90 s, plus a near-verbatim repeat of the first line at ~80% and a bookend ritual as the last line. Each beat type keeps its own visual mode (story set / cream diagram / status board / progress map), and the palette changes per act.
