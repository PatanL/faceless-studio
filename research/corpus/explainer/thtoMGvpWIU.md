### Hook

- **Hook proper: lines 1-3, 21 words, about 6 s** (0:02-0:08). Second-person "imagine" scene, a three-item "no X" list ending on "no you", then the title question.
- **Teaser stack: lines 4-6, 46 more words, 0:08-0:19.** Three items from the list, one each from the bottom (dies the first night), the middle (wipes out raccoons) and the top (the winner costs less than a sandwich). Each is a specific, odd claim the viewer can only resolve by watching to that entry. The cheap-winner teaser also adds a direct "you've probably owned one" so the viewer starts guessing.
- **Rules + thesis: 0:19-0:54.** A game frame (same four checkpoints for everyone, a check per pass), a fake-out that names the viewer's guess (teeth and claws), and a one-sentence rule the viewer is told to remember. The first entry starts at 0:54.
- Retention engine of the open: three teaser loops, one scoring loop, one guess loop and one rule the viewer is asked to apply. This is the most loaded open in the explainer corpus so far.

### Re-hook interval

- **Macro:** a new "Number N" every 47-71 s (mean about 61 s). Each entry ends on "Verdict, ..." spoken in checkpoint units, so the viewer always knows a new number is next.
- **Checkpoint micro-beats:** "Hour one / Day one / Night one / Week one" stamps appear 21 times, usually in groups of 2-3 per entry, about every 5-15 s inside an entry. They are the main pacing device: each stamp is a new mini-scene and a new chance to fail.

### Escalation ladder

Three ladders climb together:

1. **Survival time (the score):** budgie under 2 days -> guinea pig 1 day -> hamster a few days -> rabbit fails week one -> dog passes week one near people -> python all checks -> cat "forever" -> goldfish year one, year ten, 25 years. Strictly rising, so the board fills from left to right.
2. **How much wild is left (the thesis):** heavily bred (colour, calmness) -> bottlenecked genes -> wild cousin is strong but the pet is soft -> huge range inside one species (husky to pug) -> never domesticated -> self-domesticated, almost all wild -> changed only on the surface, still a carp. The rule predicts the order, which lets the viewer "play along".
3. **Impact on the world:** the pet is the victim (budgie population wiped out by sparrows) -> dogs live off people -> python wipes out mammals in a national park -> cats linked to 63 extinctions -> goldfish so big a city asks people to stop. The pivot at 5:55 (61%) is where the pet stops being the victim and becomes the threat. The moral at 9:24 then says both outcomes are bad.

Inside each entry the mini-ladder is: number + name -> origin (how humans changed it) -> checkpoint run with one joke per checkpoint -> a dated real study or "natural experiment" with exact numbers -> verdict stamp in checkpoint units. The order of the middle parts varies to stop it feeling mechanical (budgie: checkpoints first, study last; hamster: two twists before the checkpoints; rabbit and dog: a fake-out first).

### How it ends

The goldfish verdict is the only one that reaches the "1 year" checkpoint held back at 0:20, which closes the scoring loop at 93% runtime. Then a 17 s final board that groups the eight entries into three tiers (bred calm and lost in days / survives near us / stayed wild and won) and restates the thesis as "the real lesson". Then a moral twist (the losers die alone, the winners damage ecosystems), a one-line PSA (don't release pets, rehome them), and an end-screen plug that opens a new question about pets. No subscribe ask, no sponsor in the transcript.

### Pacing and device notes for the PLAYBOOK

- About 187 wpm, the fastest long-form read in the explainer corpus. Same channel as corpus #5 (145 wpm), so the channel's pace is not fixed; a straighter, stat-heavy script read faster. For Kokoro/Piper we should stay nearer 160-170 and cut words, not speed up the voice.
- The checkpoint system is a cleaner scoring device than #5's stopwatch: it is the same 3-4 stamps for every entry, it gives each entry its own mini-structure, it fills a board, and one checkpoint ("1 year") is held back so only the winner can reach it.
- A **thesis rule** the viewer is told to remember (0:50) turns a list into a test the viewer can predict. It is called back 4 times and closes the video. This is the main new device compared with #5.
- **Three-teaser open** drawn from the bottom, middle and top of the list, each specific and strange, each closed at a known later time (1:56/3:52, 6:32, 7:56).
- **Owner POV, not victim POV:** the viewer is "you" only as the owner ("your budgie", "your pug"). Second person is just 1.7% of words, but nearly every entry has one line about something owners recognise (3 a.m. wheel, bell peppers, keyboard, fair goldfish). That recognition does the job that "you are being eaten" did in #5.
- **Natural experiment framing** ("Florida ran the experiment") turns a hypothetical into documented fact. Used twice in the same state with opposite results (budgies die out, pythons take over).
- **Fake-outs** on the two "obvious" entries: rabbits (famous invaders, but those were wild ones) and dogs (wolf ancestors, but which dog?). The underdog wins (goldfish) as the final twist, set up by the "less than a sandwich" teaser.
- **Midpoint pivot at 61%:** "so far the wild is winning, but the list flips". It changes the question from "how fast does it die?" to "how much damage does it do?", which refreshes the second half.
- **Exact numbers everywhere:** about 30 dated or counted facts (1960s, 1977, 7,000, 400, 2014, 1930, 8 ft, 11, 3, 150, 2008, 1859, 24, 364, 1 in 5, 99.3%, 98.9%, 87.5%, 2013, 1.3-4 billion, 6.3-22.3 billion, 2016, 63, 10,000 years, 1.5 ft, 4 lb, 25 years). Almost all check out (see the fact check above).
- Visual grammar (inferred): a persistent survival board with four checkpoint columns that fills one row per entry; a checkpoint card (hour, day, night, week, year) for each CHK beat; a counter for every STUDY number; a split screen for every pet-vs-wild CONTRAST.

## 3. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Template: **"Same test for every contestant, ranked from first to fail to last standing"** (a countdown audited against fixed checkpoints, decided by one rule the viewer learns at the start). Length 8-10 min for 8 entries (or 60-90 s with 3 entries), about 160-170 wpm for our TTS.

1. **Cold open (0-9%).**
   1. Scene line (≤6 s): put the viewer in the role of the person responsible for the contestants (owner, keeper, builder), remove their support with a short "no X, no Y, no you" list, then ask the title question.
   2. Teaser stack (about 10 s): three teasers, one from the bottom of the list (fails almost at once), one from the middle (a strange, oddly specific effect), one from the top (the winner is the one nobody would pick, with a cheap or everyday detail). Note when each will be closed.
   3. Game rules (about 12 s): the same 3-4 checkpoints for every entry, a mark for each pass, and one checkpoint held back as a bonus level that only the winner will reach.
   4. Fake-out + thesis (about 20 s): name the viewer's likely guess, then replace it with one simple rule from research ("it's not A, it's not B, it's C"), restate it as "the more X, the faster Y", and tell the viewer to remember it.
2. **Entry block, repeated N times (each 8-12% of runtime; similar lengths; the winner may be the longest).**
   1. Number + name.
   2. Origin line: how much this contestant was changed (applies the thesis).
   3. Checkpoint run: 2-3 checkpoint stamps, each a one-line scene with one joke or one fatal gap. Vary where this sits in the entry.
   4. A real, dated study or "this place already ran the test" story with exact numbers, told as rise and fall or before and after.
   5. One owner-recognition line (something the audience has seen their own pet or object do) turned into the danger.
   6. Verdict in checkpoint units, short and quotable, written to be reused in the final board.
   7. On 3-4 entries, a seam teaser for the next entry placed before its number.
3. **Ladder rules.**
   - Order entries by how much they still match their wild or original form, so the thesis predicts the ranking and the viewer can play along.
   - Put two fake-outs on the entries the audience expects to do well (a famous relative that is not the same thing; a category with huge internal range).
   - The viewer's favorite (named in the open) is knocked out just before the midpoint.
   - At about 60%, pivot: "so far X is winning, but the list flips." The second half changes the question (from "how fast does it fail?" to "how much damage does it do?").
   - Reuse one place or one list across two entries with opposite results (same location, one dies out, one takes over; same official list, two entries on it).
   - Call the thesis back explicitly at least twice ("remember the rule?"), once on a loser and once on a winner.
   - The final entry closes the cheap-winner teaser, survives one last obstacle with a weird mechanism, and is the only one to hit the held-back bonus checkpoint.
4. **Final board (about 3%).** Group the entries into 3 tiers instead of listing all of them, and restate the thesis as the lesson.
5. **Moral + action (about 2%).** Show that both outcomes are bad (the losers suffer, the winners cause harm), then give one simple instruction.
6. **End plug (about 1.5%).** Ask a new question about the same subject and point to another video. No subscribe ask needed.

Pacing targets: a new entry every 50-70 s; a checkpoint stamp every 5-15 s inside entries; a signposted turn every 25-35 s; a joke every 15-25 s; one dated, exact study per entry; all three opening teasers closed at known points (about 20%, 65%, 80%); thesis callback at least twice; the pivot at 55-65%.
