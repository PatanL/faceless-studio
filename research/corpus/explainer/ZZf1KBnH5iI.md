Whisper spelling errors are kept verbatim in the table: Dunkleostius = Dunkleosteus, Pyrosaurus = Purussaurus, Dinosuchus = Deinosuchus, "paddled bar" = padded bar, "gouges and fossil whale ribs" = gouges in fossil whale ribs, "the top Three" (the capital comes from a segment break).

Fact check (published estimates, in pounds of force): the numbers are mostly real and conservative. Human molar bite about 150-200 lbf is fine. Lion "over 1,000" is the common popular figure. Dunkleosteus about 1,700 matches the upper end of the Anderson & Westneat lever-model estimate (about 6,000-7,400 N, roughly 1,350-1,660 lbf), and the self-sharpening bony jaw plates are correct. The saltwater crocodile 3,690 lbf is the real measured record (Erickson et al. 2012, 16,414 N), but that animal is usually reported as about 17 ft, not 15 ft (the source or whisper may be wrong). T. rex "up to about 7,800" is the low estimate (Gignac & Erickson 2017, about 34,500 N); other models go to about 12,800 lbf, so "up to" is misleading. Purussaurus "about three trucks" (about 69,000 N, about 15,500 lbf) and a skull of about 1.4 m (about 4.6 ft) are right. Deinosuchus "nearly five trucks" (about 102,800 N, about 23,100 lbf) and bite marks on tyrannosaurid bones are right. Megalodon "up to about 41,000" is the top of the Wroe et al. 2008 range (108,500-182,200 N, about 24,400-41,000 lbf). The truck unit is implied at about 4,600 lb (41,000 / 9), which is about right for a full-size pickup, and the video keeps that unit consistent all the way through. The only real stretch is the hook: "bite straight through nine pickup trucks" turns a force equal to nine trucks' weight into crushing nine trucks, which is not the same thing. Our scripts must keep that line honest ("as much force as N trucks resting on one tooth").

- **Hook length:** about 4 s and 16 words (0:00-0:04): one question that names the category (prehistoric bite) and gives away the final number (nine pickup trucks) but holds back the animal. With the "you" baseline it is 12 s and 43 words (13%). There is no cold open or teaser shot. The hook works by giving a known target number and hiding who reaches it.
- **Re-hook interval:** two layers. (a) Micro: a new animal (rung) about every 10 s (8 rungs from 0:04 to 1:13, gaps of 4-17 s), and a new number about every 5 s. (b) Macro: short "even/still" surprise lines about every 15-20 s: "didn't even have teeth" (0:16, 18%), "strongest ever actually measured" (0:28, 31%), "still doesn't even make the top three" (0:52, 58%), "every winner lives in water" (0:55, 61%), "bit even harder" (1:06, 73%), "but Megalodon beat every one" (1:13, 81%). Each one is 4-12 words and comes right after a long number sentence. The strongest re-hook sits at 58%, exactly where the viewer's guess (T. rex) is proved wrong.
- **Open loops:** one main loop. L1 "which animal reaches nine trucks?" opens at 0:00 with the number already given, is narrowed at 0:52 ("top three": three rungs left) and at 0:55 (it lives in water), and closes at 1:22 (91%) on the same number. A small loop opens at 0:16 (how do you bite with no teeth?) and closes within 10 s.
- **Escalation ladder:** force climbs about 2x per rung: 200, 1,000, 1,700, 3,690, 7,800, about 15,500, about 23,000, 41,000 lbf. The comparison object changes at first (a man on a tooth, piano, cow) and then locks onto one unit once the numbers get big (most of a truck, 1.5, 3, nearly 5, almost 9). The source of the number climbs too: everyday feel, popular figure, "actually measured", "estimated", "scientists calculate". Habitat flips from land to water at 61%. Each fossil rung follows the same pattern: size, number, fossil evidence.
- **Ranking honesty trick:** the croc is called the strongest bite "ever measured", so the later estimated numbers do not contradict it. The video separates measured from estimated without slowing down to explain it.
- **Body anchor:** only one second-person line (rung 0), but it sets the scale for the whole video: every later number is a multiple of your own bite. After that the body anchor comes from objects the viewer knows (piano, cow, truck, a hand next to a tooth).
- **How it ends:** the winner is named, then its proof, then the number from the hook. No CTA, no subscribe ask, no closing opinion. The last phrase repeats the first line, so the video loops into a rewatch.
- **Pacing note for us:** about 189 wpm is fast for Kokoro/Piper. Ours should run about 165-180 wpm in 60-75 s, or keep 90 s and cut about 30 words. Long and/and/and sentences work here because each clause adds one fact. For TTS we should split them into two sentences per rung and keep the 4-9 word punch lines whole.
- **Visual lesson (inferred):** one rung equals one stage: an animal card, a force meter that keeps climbing in a corner, and a row of truck icons that fill up toward the nine shown in the hook. The truck row is the progress device (PLAYBOOK "visible progress device"), so the viewer always sees how close the next rung is to the target. Proof beats get one fossil close-up each. The land-to-water turn is a good place for a full-screen transition (water line, color change).

## 3. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Template: **"Known target, hidden winner"** (ascending stat ladder with one repeated unit). Length 60-90 s, about 180-280 words at about 170-185 wpm, one rung per 8-10 s, 7-9 rungs.

1. **Target question (0-4 s, about 4%).** Ask the title question as a superlative, and give away the record number in a vivid everyday unit, but not the holder. Visual: the full unit row (for example, N icons), all empty.
2. **You as rung 0 (about 9%).** The viewer's own value for the same stat, shown with a body picture (weight on a tooth, a lungful, a sprint). This is the only second-person line, and it sets the scale.
3. **Familiar modern rung (about 4%).** A well-known living animal, about 5x you, compared with a heavy household object.
4. **Paradox rung (about 13%).** Open with a short "this one didn't even have [the expected part]" line, then explain the odd mechanism and give the number with a bigger object comparison.
5. **Measured record rung (about 13%).** The strongest value ever measured in a living animal, with the experiment in one clause. Switch to the hook's unit here ("most of one [unit]").
6. **Expected winner rung (about 17%).** The most famous animal: number in units, one mechanism detail, one fun fossil proof. Then the main re-hook (about 55-60%): "and it still doesn't make the top three." That breaks the viewer's guess and sets a countdown.
7. **Reordering rule (about 2%, one short line).** One rule that all the top three share (habitat, era, body plan). Visual: a full-screen setting change.
8. **#3 (about 10%).** Size first, then the estimated number in units (about 2x the expected winner).
9. **#2 (about 8%).** A 3-5 word "even more" punch line, then size, units, and a fossil proof that ties back to an earlier rung (it attacked or ate that animal).
10. **#1 reveal (about 19%).** Name it with a "but" in mid-sentence, give a body-scale size comparison, a fossil proof, then the science source and the hook's exact number. No CTA. The last words repeat the hook's unit so the video loops.

Rules to carry over:
- Give away the record number in the hook and hide only the holder. The unit row on screen is the progress bar.
- Each rung is about 2x the last. Change comparison objects early, then lock onto one unit once numbers pass about 3,000.
- Every fossil rung follows size, number, physical evidence. Put the evidence (bite marks, crushed bone in fossil dung, gouged bones) on screen.
- Keep "measured" and "estimated" apart in the wording, so the ranking never contradicts itself.
- Put a 4-12 word "even/still" re-hook after every long number sentence, and the biggest one where the obvious winner loses (about 55-60%).
- Use real numbers with sources, and keep the hook claim honest: "as much force as N [units]", never "bites through N [units]".
