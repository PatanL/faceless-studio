## 2. Structure summary

### Beat sequence with share of runtime (37.5 s, 94 words)

| Beat | Time | Seconds | % of runtime | Words |

| Struggle + environment rung (treading, current, cold) | 0:11-0:18 | 7 | 19% | 18 |

Shape: a three-act survival micro-story. Act 1 (0-29%) puts him alone in the water, Act 2 (29-77%) makes it worse in three rungs and peaks in one action, Act 3 (77-100%) rescues him fast. The rising part is about two-thirds of the runtime and the release is only the last quarter.

### Hook

- Length: 11 words, about 6 s (one sentence). If the abandonment line is counted as part of the hook (it is what makes the fall a survival story), it is 23 words in 11 s.
- Type: in medias res. Disaster in the first two words after the subject, no title restatement, no question, no "you". The title ("Man Lost Floating In Ocean") is already the premise, so the hook skips straight to the event.
- Hooks inside the hook: a mystery word ("mysteriously"), an isolation clause ("without anyone noticing"), and a strong visual (a body falling off a ship hull, then sinking).

### Re-hook interval

There are no explicit verbal re-hooks ("but it gets worse", questions). Re-hooking is done by **time-jump openers**, each paired with a hard cut to a new camera angle:
- 0:06 "When he surfaced" (16%)
- 0:18 "Hours later" (48%)
- 0:27 "Finally, after nearly 20 ... hours" (72%)

That is about every 9-12 s for the time jumps. Underneath them, a new threat or event lands every 3-6 s (fall, ship leaving, currents/cold, bump, fin, kick, rescue ship, helicopter), and the camera angle changes about every 4 s. Captions change about every 1 s.

### Open loops

| Loop | Opens | Closes | Span |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: How did he fall, "mysteriously"? | 0:00 | Never (deliberately unresolved; comment bait and a reason to rewatch) | open |

| C: What was the "something"? | 0:18 | 0:21 "a fin" | 3 s micro loop |

Pattern: one long survival loop held for about 80% of the runtime, with two quick micro loops nested in the middle so the viewer gets small closures while waiting for the big one. One loop is never closed.

### Escalation ladder

1. Fall, unseen (accident + isolation).
2. Ship leaves (rescue is moving away; "already").
3. Body threats from the sea: currents, then cold.
4. Time: hours pass (endurance).
5. Contact with an unseen thing (touch, noun withheld).
6. Predator revealed (fin, circling).
7. Confrontation (he kicks) -> threat leaves.
8. Time peak: the one number, ~20 hours, recasts the whole ordeal.
9. Release: ship spots him -> helicopter -> full recovery.

The ladder climbs on three axes at once: isolation (crowd -> alone -> a dot in the sea from above), threat (gravity -> water -> cold -> animal) and time (seconds -> hours -> 20 hours). The predator rung is the peak and sits at 48-64%.

### How it ends

A fast, flat happy ending: rescue in three short clauses over 10 s, then "a full recovery", with no lesson, no fact, no CTA. The rescue mirrors the opening (a ship lost him, a ship found him). The visual grade shifts from cold blue to warm amber one beat before the word "finally", so the eye knows he is saved before the ear. The video cuts about 1 s after the last word and loops to the falling shot; the unresolved "mysteriously" makes the replay feel like a second look for clues.

### Pacing and device notes for the PLAYBOOK

- Slow and clear: about 150 wpm, captions of 1-3 words changing every second. The hook is the slowest part (about 110 wpm). This is a story Short, not a fact-dump Short; compare Casual Geographic at about 288 wpm.
- Third person, past tense, a nameless man. Zero "you", zero questions, one number. The viewer is put in his place by the camera (over-the-shoulder, frontal close-up), not by the pronoun.
- Withholding nouns ("something", "creature", "fin" without "shark") creates micro loops and lets the visual say the scary word.
- Every sentence starts with a time or sequence marker (When / Hours later / Finally) or "He [verb]": very easy to follow at a glance.
- Camera angle is a storytelling device: top-down = isolation and danger; over-the-shoulder = abandonment; eye level = agency; rescuer POV = safety. Colour grade = mood (cold teal = danger, warm amber = rescue).

## 3. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Template for a 30-45 s vertical "lone survivor" story Short (one person, one hostile environment, one predator or hazard, one rescue). At about 150 wpm, 38 s is about 90-100 words. Third person or second person both work; this reference uses third person plus camera POV for immersion.

1. **Inciting incident, 0-15% (10-12 words, about 5-6 s, delivered slowly).** The accident happens in the first clause. Add one unexplained adverb (how it happened is a mystery) and one isolation clause (nobody saw). Visual: wide shot of the accident, then the character swallowed by the environment.
2. **Abandonment stake, 15-30% (10-13 words).** Start with a "when [he/you] [recovered]" marker. The only way out (vehicle, group, shelter) is already leaving or gone. Visual: over-the-shoulder shot with the escape route shrinking in the distance.
3. **Struggle and environment rung, 30-48% (15-20 words).** The character does the basic survival action, then two environmental threats are named in one clause (motion + temperature, for example). Visual: frontal close-up; the face reacts; the character briefly goes under or loses footing.
4. **Time jump + unseen contact, 48-56% (8-10 words).** Open with a time-jump phrase. The character feels something they cannot see; withhold the noun. Visual: hard cut to a top-down or far shot where the character is tiny; the hazard enters the corner of the frame on the key word.
5. **Predator/hazard reveal, 56-64% (5-7 words).** Name only a part of the threat (a fin, a shadow, eyes), moving in a circling or approaching pattern. Visual: the hazard moves closer each second.
6. **Confrontation and turn, 64-72% (10-12 words, two short clauses).** The character acts once against the threat; the threat retreats. Keep it to about 3 s. Visual: back to eye level, splash/impact.
7. **Second time jump + the one number, 72-85% (12-15 words).** Relief marker plus the total ordeal length as the single number in the script. A chance rescuer appears that mirrors the escape route from step 2. Visual: change the colour grade from cold to warm one beat before the relief word; the rescuer's lights in the distance; the character signals.
8. **Extraction and outcome, 85-100% (10-13 words).** The rescue from the rescuer's point of view, then a flat one-clause outcome ("recovered", "made it home"). No CTA, no lesson. Cut within 1 s of the last word so the loop restarts on the incident; the unexplained adverb from step 1 stays open.

Pacing constants: about 150 wpm; a caption of 1-3 words per second; a new camera angle about every 4 s; a new event or threat every 3-6 s; three time-jump openers at about 15%, 48% and 72%; one long survival loop plus two nested micro loops of 3-4 s each, placed at 48-70%; one unresolved mystery; one number, placed late; a cold-to-warm colour change at about 75% as the visual cue for rescue.

For our code-only kit: the camera language maps to 2D layers easily. Top-down = a small figure in a large dark field with a fin shape sliding along an arc; over-the-shoulder = a back-of-head silhouette in the foreground with a shrinking ship sprite; the colour change = a palette tween on the background gradient; rescuer POV = a foreground skid/rotor silhouette over a tiny figure.
