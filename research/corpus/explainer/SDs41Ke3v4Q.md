Fact check (from the published study and standard references, not re-checked online in this pass; confirm before any of our scripts borrows a claim): the experiment is McClain, Nunnally & Benfield 2019, "Alligators in the abyss" (PLOS ONE), three carcasses placed at about 2,000 m in the northern Gulf of Mexico in early 2019. The giant isopods (Bathynomus giganteus) inside the first carcass within about a day, the skeleton with Osedax worms on the second at about 50 days, and the third carcass missing with its weight dragged away are all as reported. The weight figures agree with each other (20 kg is about 44 lb, said as "45 pound"). The drag distance ("50, 60 ft") is the scientist's recollection; check the paper's figure before quoting a number. Osedax borings in Mesozoic marine reptile fossils (plesiosaur bones) are published (Danise & Higgs 2015). Bluntnose sixgill sharks reach about 5-5.5 m and are recorded to roughly 2,000-2,500 m, so the suspect fits; the video says itself that nobody saw what took it. The "63 km offshore" live alligator record and the "about a million alligators in Louisiana" figure were not checked here. The NOAA clip of a deep-sea grouper (wreckfish) swallowing a dogfish at a swordfish carcass is real footage. The video is careful with uncertainty: "most parsimonious idea", "we have no idea", "all we really know". Our scripts should copy that care.

### Escalation ladder

Three identical carcasses, three different fates, each stranger than the last:
- **Carcass 1 (32%):** many visible small eaters, fast (inside within a day, 13 isopods). Question answered: yes, animals get through the armor.
- **Carcass 2 (53%):** almost gone after about 50 days, but the bones are alive with tiny worms, a species new to science, with a link to sea reptiles millions of years ago. The stakes move from "it gets eaten" to "new species and deep time".
- **Carcass 3 (72%):** nothing left. Clues (divot, drag mark, cut rope, 20 kg weight moved), one suspect ruled out, a huge shark as the likely answer, and nobody saw it.

What the viewer can see drops at each rung (swarm on camera, then tiny worms, then nothing), while the size of the eater rises (isopods, inch-long worms, then a 5 m shark). The final rung is the biggest animal and the one never seen, which is why it ends the video.

## 3. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Template: **"One real experiment, three outcomes"** (true-story mystery box). Length 12-18 min, about 2,000-2,800 words at 165-175 wpm. Works for any real study where identical samples had different fates (three bait drops, three tagged animals, three probes, three seeds).

1. **Plain-fact cold open, 0-2%.** One dated sentence: who did what odd thing, where, and how far down or how long. Follow it with a short flat line that implies "and nobody knew what would happen". Then state where the answers come from (the published study, the team's footage) and open two loops: what happened to each sample, and why anyone would do it, with "why" framed as the bigger question.
2. **Reframe and science stakes, 2-5%.** Admit it sounds absurd, then give the serious reason: one real gap in what scientists can measure, explained with one plain comparison. End on the title question, asked as a consequence of that gap.
3. **Three-question roadmap, 5-7%.** List three set-up questions (why this sample, how did they get it, how did they place it). Show them as numbered cards, and check each one off as it is answered.
4. **Set-up sections, 7-30% (three sections of 50-100 s each).** Each answers one roadmap question with one real number, one human or comic detail from the team's account (paraphrased and attributed), and one ethics or "it's not what you think" aside. In the placement section, plant 2-3 physical objects (tether, weight, marker) that will return as clues. Close with "here's what they wanted to test", stated as a guessable either/or.
5. **Stakes restated, 30-32%.** Repeat the gap from step 2 in nearly the same words, then the bridge: "for this to matter, something down there has to eat it."
6. **Outcome 1, 32-42%.** Counted opening ("when they returned to the first..."), the eater left unnamed for one sentence, then a held silent shot. Then speed, count, name, how it got past the defense, extra guests, and a one-line takeaway in the expert's position.
7. **Wider-lesson tangent, 42-53%.** One concept (how the energy spreads), one filmed side story in which the viewer's guess about the top predator is broken, then the "we only ever see snapshots" limit. Plant the narrator's own tool or method here. Close with an explicit "back to the [samples]".
8. **Outcome 2, 53-72%.** Parallel opening, a time jump, a "but it wasn't empty" turn line, and a slow reveal ending on the punch word. Raise the stakes with "new to science" or "first ever recorded here". Ask the expert's open question and give the honest "nobody knows". Close the loop planted in step 4 aloud ("this loops back to...") with deep-time evidence. End on a "we don't know X, but we do know Y" pair.
9. **Outcome 3, 72-83%.** "Nowhere to be found", three short lines of nothing, then a detective sequence: the site is confirmed, the planted objects found as clues, one suspect ruled out, the prime suspect given means, opportunity and motive with hedges, and the narrator admitting nobody saw it.
10. **Synthesis, 83-89%.** Identical samples, different fates. Three objections, each struck in one short line. A partial, hedged answer to the step 2 gap, then three unknowns ending with the title question itself.
11. **Mission outro, 89-98%.** "We weren't there to see it" becomes the reason for the next project: the obstacles (pressure, power, cold) and a planned next deployment with a dated risk tied to a planted object.
12. **CTA, 98-100%.** Point to where the next result appears, and ask viewers to comment their guess for the unsolved outcome.

Rules to carry over:
- State the odd true event as fact in the first sentence; the oddness is the hook, so no question is needed until the stakes are set.
- Give the set-up a visible roadmap, so a long set-up (about 25%) still feels like progress.
- Space the outcome reveals 3-3.5 min apart (about 32%, 53%, 72%), each opened by the same counted sentence frame and a held silent shot.
- Order outcomes so the eater gets bigger while what we can see shrinks, and save the unseen one for last.
- Plant one physical object early and pay it off as a clue and again in the outro.
- Hedge every claim the study cannot prove, and say aloud what is unknown. Leave the last mystery open and turn it into the comment prompt.
- We have no interviewee: use attributed paraphrase of the published study and the team's public statements, never invented quotes.
