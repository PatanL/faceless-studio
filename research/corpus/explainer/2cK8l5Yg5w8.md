Fact check (done for this annotation; do it again for any of our scripts that borrow a claim):
- **Sugar:** the rat oral LD50 of sucrose is about 30 g/kg, so about 2 kg for a 70 kg adult, which is about 500 teaspoons at 4 g each. The number holds. The blue-skin / seizure / stopped-breathing chain is a dramatized picture, not a documented human case.
- **Cinnamon:** cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which can harm the liver in a small group of sensitive people. Correct, and the video says itself that it is rare.
- **Saffron:** safranal has sedative effects in animal studies. Saffron is toxic at about 5 g and reported lethal at about 12-20 g. Correct in kind.
- **Salt:** a soy sauce bottle holds enough salt for severe hypernatremia (there is a published case of a young man who died after drinking about 1 L). Water leaving brain cells shrinks the brain and can tear vessels, which causes bleeding. Correct.
- **Bananas:** potassium-40 emits a positron in about 0.001% of decays. A banana has about 15 Bq of K-40, so it gives off about 4,000-5,000 positrons a year. 3 ng of positrons is about 3.3 x 10^18 particles, which needs about 700 trillion banana-years by this estimate. The video says 437 trillion, which is the same order of magnitude (it depends on the potassium and branching figures used). 3 ng of positrons plus 3 ng of electrons gives about 540 kJ, about 130 g of TNT, so "a hole in your chest" is fair. In reality the positrons annihilate at once and could never be collected; the "literally" and "grenade" wording marks this as a joke hypothetical. Our scripts must say when a step is impossible in practice.

## 2. Structure summary

Beat sequence with share of the 67 s runtime:

| Beat | Time | Seconds | % of runtime |
|---|---|---|---|
| COLD OPEN (you are already dead, which item?) | 0:00-0:02 | 2 | 3% |
| SUSPECT 1: sugar (flip, dose, three-step body failure, doubt dismissal) | 0:02-0:16 | 14 | 21% |

| SUSPECT 3: saffron (question cut, superlative, sedative, dose contrast) | 0:26-0:36 | 10 | 15% |
| SUSPECT 4: salt (price joke dismissal, one bottle, three-step brain chain) | 0:36-0:44 | 8 | 12% |

| PAYOFF: antimatter dose and annihilation | 0:49-1:05 | 16 | 24% |
| VERDICT ("case closed") | 1:05-1:07 | 2 | 3% |

- **Hook length:** about 2 s and 6 words (line 1). If you count the first suspect's claim as part of the hook, about 5 s and 16 words (0:00-0:05). The hook is the outcome, said in past tense to "you": a death has happened and the viewer has to find the cause. There is no "here's why" or teaser of the answer. The answer stays hidden until 0:44 (66%).
- **Re-hook interval:** a new suspect about every 10 s (0:02, 0:16, 0:26, 0:36, 0:44; average 10.5 s across the four changes). Each change is set off by a short dismissal or question line (8, 9, 3 and 6 words) placed right after a mechanism line, which is where a viewer might think "is that it?". The suspects get shorter each time (14, 10, 10, 8 s), so the pace speeds up into the twist. After the twist there is no re-hook; the 16 s payoff runs on curiosity about how a banana could kill you.
- **Open loops:** one loop. L1 "which kitchen item killed you?" opens at 0:00, is re-opened by each dismissal (0:13, 0:23, 0:36), and closes at 0:44 (66%). Each suspect is a false answer, not a sub-loop. The crime frame (killed, innocent, victims, cheaper, killer, case closed) is a second, lighter thread that only closes on the last line.
- **Escalation ladder:** two ladders. (a) Believability goes up across the suspects: an impossible amount of sugar, a rare liver reaction, an expensive spice, then one ordinary bottle of soy sauce, the most likely real danger. (b) Then the twist breaks the ladder on purpose with the least likely item and the most violent outcome (a hole in your chest). Dose numbers move from household units (teaspoons, bottle) to science units and huge counts (nanograms, trillions of bananas), so the numbers get bigger exactly as the claim gets sillier.
- **Repeated suspect slot:** every suspect fills the same 4 slots in the same order: name (with a flip or a superlative), compound or mechanism, dose, dismissal. The body-damage lists of three (line 3, line 12, line 15) are the same device used three times, never with the same words. The twist reuses the slots (dose at 0:49, body damage at 0:59), which is why it feels like a real answer and not a random joke.
- **Body anchor:** "you" in 8 of 16 lines. Each suspect ends on what happens to your body (blood, liver, brain, brain again, chest). The viewer stays the victim throughout.
- **How it ends:** a two-word verdict that closes the crime frame from line 1. No CTA, no subscribe ask, no outro.
- **Pacing note for us:** about 183 wpm in 67 s is a good target for a 60-70 s list Short and works with Kokoro/Piper if the dismissal lines get a short pause before them. 200 words is enough for 4 suspects plus a twist; 5 suspects would need about 80 s, or shorter mechanism lines.
- **Visual lesson (inferred):** one object per suspect, shown as a "line-up" card, then one body diagram for the mechanism. In code: a suspect card (object sprite + name), a dose counter, a body silhouette where one organ lights up, and a stamp or X over the card on each dismissal. The twist gets a spotlight and a different colour, and the end gets a "case closed" stamp.

## 3. Transferable skeleton (no original wording)

Template: **"Suspect line-up with an absurd twist"** (second-person whodunit list). Length 55-75 s, about 170-220 words at about 180 wpm, 4 suspects at about 8-14 s each, then a twist and a payoff of about 20 s.

1. **Past-tense cold open (0-2 s, about 3%).** One sentence of 5-7 words: an everyday place or set of objects has already done something terrible to "you". It opens one question: which one? Visual: the place plus a crime-scene cue.
2. **Suspect 1, the most harmless item (about 20%).** Name it with a "seems safe, but" turn. Give a dose in a household unit, then a three-step body failure in one sentence. Drop it with a self-objection question about whether anyone could really take that much.
3. **Suspect 2 (about 15%).** Change scene with "or it could be..." and no transition. Name one real compound, give a one-step mechanism, and drop it for a different reason (only rare people, a rare condition).
4. **Suspect 3 (about 15%).** Change scene with a 2-4 word question. Open with a superlative fact about the item, then the mechanism, then a low-dose vs high-dose contrast.
5. **Suspect 4, the most believable one (about 12%).** Bring it in with a single joke line that also drops the last suspect (cost, effort, practicality). Give the scariest realistic danger: a single everyday container is enough, with a three-step body chain.
6. **Twist (about 7%, at 60-70%).** "But the real answer is..." the least likely item, said straight-faced, with a superlative. Answer first, explain after.
7. **Payoff (about 25%).** Fill the same slots as the suspects: dose (a tiny-sounding amount, then a huge count of the item, framed with a comic understatement), then the body damage, the most violent one in the video. State plainly when a step is impossible in practice.
8. **Verdict (last 3%).** Two or three words that close the frame from line 1. No CTA.

Rules to carry over:
- Every suspect gets the same slots in the same order (name, mechanism, dose, dismissal). Viewers learn the pattern by suspect 2, and the twist pays off because it uses the same slots.
- Every dismissal gives a new reason (dose, rare people, cost). Never dismiss two suspects the same way.
- Put the most believable suspect last before the twist, so the twist breaks a rising ladder.
- Keep the frame words (crime, case, verdict, or whatever frame we choose) going through the whole script, and close it in the last line.
- Short dismissal or question lines (3-9 words) right after each mechanism line, about every 10 s.
- Check every dose and number before writing (they are the credibility engine); flag joke hypotheticals as impossible in practice.
- Keep the body as the anchor: each suspect ends on a named organ or symptom in "you".
