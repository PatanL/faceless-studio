The giant Pacific octopus is the largest octopus species: spread wide, its arms reach past twice your height. It has no bones, only a
parrot-like beak, so any gap wider than that beak is a way out. Over two thousand suckers taste whatever they grab, its skin matches
the rock behind it in under a second, and most of its nerve cells sit in its arms.
In 2010, at the Seattle Aquarium, two people in identical uniforms visited eight of these octopuses. One always brought food, the other
prodded them with a bristly stick. Within about two weeks, the octopuses treated them differently.
It doesn't want a fight, though. Bites on people are rare, and it only lives three to five years.

Facts and sources:
- Largest octopus species (*Enteroctopus dofleini*); the seven-arm octopus may rival it on one partial specimen. Smithsonian Ocean, "Giant Pacific Octopus"; Seattle Aquarium.
- Big adults span over ~4 m, more than twice a 1.75 m person (records ~6 m are rare). Seattle Aquarium; Monterey Bay Aquarium.
- No bones; the beak is the only hard part, so it fits through any gap larger than the beak. Monterey Bay Aquarium; Seattle Aquarium.
- About 280 suckers per arm, over 2,000 in total. Seattle Aquarium; Oceana.
- Suckers carry chemoreceptors and "taste" by touch. van Giesen et al. 2020, *Cell* 183(3): 594-604.
- Skin colour and texture change in well under a second (chromatophores, papillae). Messenger 2001, *Biological Reviews* 76: 473-528.
- Venomous saliva paralyses crabs and other prey. Oceana; Monterey Bay Aquarium.
- Roughly two-thirds of an octopus's nerve cells are in the arms (data mainly from *Octopus vulgaris*). Hochner 2012, *Current Biology* 22(20): R887-R892.
- 2010 study: eight octopuses, two people in identical uniforms (one fed, one touched them with a bristly stick); within about two weeks they responded differently to each. Anderson, Mather, Monette and Zimsen 2010, *Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science* 13(3): 261-272. The study shows recognition of individuals, not which cue (face or body) they used.
- Bites on humans are rare; it escapes with ink and jetting. Seattle Aquarium; Oceana.
- Lives about three to five years; a female stops eating while guarding her eggs for months and dies soon after they hatch. Seattle Aquarium; NOAA Alaska Fisheries Science Center.
