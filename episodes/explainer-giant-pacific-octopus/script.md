## Facts to verify

- Giant Pacific octopus (*Enteroctopus dofleini*) is generally described as the largest octopus species. (Caveat: the seven-arm octopus *Haliphron atlanticus* may rival it by a mass estimate from one partial specimen; on screen "BIGGEST OCTOPUS" follows the standard description.) Source: Smithsonian Ocean, "Giant Pacific Octopus"; Seattle Aquarium species page.
- Big adults can have an arm span over ~4 m, more than twice a 1.75 m person. Records reach ~6 m but are rare; we claim only "past twice your height". Source: Seattle Aquarium "Giant Pacific octopus"; Monterey Bay Aquarium species page.
- No bones; the beak is the only hard part, so it can squeeze through any gap larger than its beak. Source: Monterey Bay Aquarium "Giant Pacific octopus"; Seattle Aquarium.
- About 280 suckers per arm, over two thousand in total. Source: Seattle Aquarium; Oceana "Giant Pacific Octopus".
- Octopus suckers carry chemoreceptors, so they "taste" by touch. Source: van Giesen et al. 2020, *Cell* 183(3): 594-604 (octopus chemotactile receptors).
- Skin colour (and texture) changes in well under a second via chromatophores and papillae. Source: Messenger 2001, *Biological Reviews* 76: 473-528, "Cephalopod chromatophores".
- Venomous saliva paralyses crabs and other prey. Source: Oceana species page; Monterey Bay Aquarium.
- In octopuses, roughly two-thirds of nerve cells are in the arms (data mainly from *Octopus vulgaris*; stated only as "most"). Source: Hochner 2012, *Current Biology* 22(20): R887-R892, "An embodied view of octopus neurobiology".
- 2010 study: eight giant Pacific octopuses at the Seattle Aquarium; two people in identical uniforms, one always fed them, one always touched them with a bristly stick; within about two weeks the octopuses responded differently to each. Source: Anderson, Mather, Monette and Zimsen 2010, *Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science* 13(3): 261-272, "Octopuses (*Enteroctopus dofleini*) recognize individual humans". The study shows recognition of individuals, not which cue (face vs body) they used, so the script says "who you are", never "your face".
- Bites on humans are rare; it is shy and escapes with ink and jetting. Source: Seattle Aquarium; Oceana.
- Lifespan about three to five years. A female stops eating while guarding her eggs for months (about six in cold water) and dies soon after they hatch. Source: Seattle Aquarium "Giant Pacific octopus"; NOAA Alaska Fisheries Science Center species profile.

[music: deep_ocean] A giant Pacific octopus can learn who you are. [sfx: sonar_ping]

Not people in general. You, and whether you fed it or poked it.

It's the largest octopus species, and spread wide, its arms reach past twice your height. [sfx: whoosh]

In under a second its skin matches the rock behind you. Its saliva carries a venom that leaves a crab limp. [sfx: whoosh_long]

But that's still not the strangest part. [pause: 0.3] Most of its nerve cells sit in its arms, so each arm can explore alone. [sfx: riser]

Back to you. In two thousand ten, at the Seattle Aquarium, two people dressed alike visited eight of these octopuses.

One always brought food. The other prodded them with a bristly stick. Within two weeks, the octopuses treated them differently. [sfx: impact]

So say you're the stick person. Disguise? Matching clothes didn't fool them. Locked tank? Remember the door. The dark? Its arms taste you. [sfx: tick]

And it hasn't long to hold a grudge. It lives three to five years, and a mother stops eating to guard her eggs, then dies as they hatch.

So be the one with the food. For the few years it gets, it knows who you are.
