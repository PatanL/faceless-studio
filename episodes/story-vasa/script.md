# The Warship That Sank in Its Own Harbour (Short, story lane)

**Premise.** In summer 1628, before the new Swedish warship Vasa sails, Captain Söfring Hansson shows Vice Admiral
Klas Fleming a stability test: thirty men run across the upper deck. After three runs the ship rolls so far that
Fleming stops the test. The king, Gustavus Adolphus, is at war in Poland and writes to get the ship to sea. On
10 August 1628 she sails in front of a crowd, heels in a gust, rights, heels again until the open lower gun ports
go under, floods and sinks about 1,300 m out; about 30 people die. The inquiry punishes no one. The cause: too much
weight high up (two gun decks of heavy bronze guns), too little ballast low down, and no room to add more without
bringing the lower gun ports to the waterline. Raised in 1961, now in the Vasa Museum, Stockholm.

## Facts to verify (checked 2026-11-02)

- Stability test, summer 1628: 30 men ran across the upper deck; Fleming stopped it after three trips, fearing she
  would capsize; the ship's master testified that Fleming said he wished the king were at home. (Wikipedia, Vasa
  (ship), citing the 1628 inquest; Vasa Museum)
- Gustavus Adolphus was leading the army in Poland and sent letters urging that she be put to sea as soon as
  possible. On 5 August 1626 he ordered seventy-two 24-pound guns, too many for one gun deck; she was built with two
  gun decks. (Wikipedia; the "late second deck" story is a myth and is not used)
- Hundreds of carvings (about 500 sculptures) cover the ship. (Vasa Museum)
- 10 August 1628: four sails set; in full view of a crowd of hundreds, if not thousands; a gust heeled her, she
  righted, a second gust pushed the open lower gun ports under; the lower gun deck flooded; she sank about 1,300 m
  out; about 30 died. (Wikipedia; Vasa Museum audioguide "The shipwreck")
- Inquiry 5 September 1628 found no one guilty; Arendt de Groote, of the builders' side, answered "Only God knows".
  (Wikipedia)
- About 120 tonnes of stone ballast; experts estimate she needed about twice that, but more weight would have
  brought the lower gun ports too close to the water. (Vasa Museum FAQ and exhibit text)
- The guns were bronze 24-pounders. Raised in 1961 (333 years later), about 95 % original; in the Vasa Museum,
  Stockholm, since 1990. (Wikipedia; Vasa Museum)
- Hedges on screen: "about thirty", "about twice". The comparison "like a weighted toy" is ours.
